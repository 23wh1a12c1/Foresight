01   Engagement Overview 
 
Project FORESIGHT is a four-week data science engagement. You are not completing a 
coursework assignment — you are the data scientist Zidio has placed on a client account, and 
you are expected to behave like one: understand the client’s business, deliver analysis they can 
act on, and present it so a non-technical decision-maker trusts it. 
The client, NorthBay Living, is a mid-size direct-to-consumer home & lifestyle brand. They sell 
online, they hold inventory, and right now they plan that inventory on gut feel and spreadsheets. 
They are losing money in two directions at once: best-sellers run out (lost sales) and slow movers 
pile up (cash locked in stock that later gets marked down). Your job is to turn their own data into a 
demand forecast and an early-warning system that tells the planning team what to reorder, what to 
clear, and what to leave alone. 
 
1.1  What you will deliver 
By the end of the engagement you will hand the client a working forecasting and risk system, a 
planning dashboard, a deployed scoring service, and an executive readout. The full deliverable list 
and acceptance criteria are in Sections 04 and 09.

06   Solution Architecture 
 
FORESIGHT is a straightforward analytics stack: raw extracts flow through a reproducible pipeline 
into models, and the models’ outputs are served to stakeholders through a dashboard and a scoring 
service. Keep the layers separate and documented so the client can re-run and trust the whole thing.

6.1  Principles 
• Reproducible: the whole pipeline re-runs from raw data with one command; no manual 
spreadsheet steps. 
• Honest: every model is compared against a baseline on a fair backtest before it is trusted. 
• Usable: outputs reach stakeholders as a dashboard and a service, not only as notebooks. 
• Documented: assumptions, data issues, and limitations are written down, not left implicit. 
07   Methodology & Modelling Approach 
 
Follow a disciplined workflow rather than jumping to a complex model. The order below is deliberate: 
you define how you will measure success, build a baseline, and only then try to beat it. This is how 
professional forecasting work avoids fooling itself.

7.1  Step detail 
Stage What you do 
Frame & metric Fix the forecast horizon and the accuracy metric (WAPE, with bias as a 
secondary check) before modelling. 
Baseline Build a seasonal-naive forecast. This is the bar every later model must clear. 
Features Engineer lag features, rolling statistics, calendar/seasonality, and promotion 
signals. 
Model Train a forecasting model (e.g., gradient-boosted trees or a classical time-series 
method). 
Backtest Evaluate with rolling-origin cross-validation — never a single random split for time 
series. 
Evaluate & select Compare to the baseline on backtest. If it doesn’t win, ship the baseline and say 
why. 
Risk score Combine the forecast with inventory position to score stockout and overstock risk. 
The non-negotiable rule of this engagement 
Beat the baseline, honestly. Forecasting is uniquely easy to fake by leaking future information or testing on 
the training period. Use rolling-origin backtesting, never let future data touch a feature, and report WAPE 
against the baseline in your readme. A model that can’t beat seasonal-naive is not a failure to hide — it is a 
finding to report.

08   Risk Scoring & Decisioning 
 
A forecast on its own does not tell the ops team what to do. The risk layer converts the forecast plus 
the current inventory position into a decision for every SKU. This is the part the client actually acts 
on, so it must be transparent and explainable.

8.1  How risk is scored 
• Stockout risk: compare forecast demand over the lead time against on-hand plus on-order 
stock. If projected stock falls below a safety level, the SKU is at risk of stocking out. 
• Overstock risk: compare on-hand stock against forecast demand over a forward window. If you 
are holding far more than you will sell, the SKU is overstocked. 
• Each SKU gets a risk level and a recommended action; the rupee value at stake is attached so 
the team can prioritise. 
8.2  The decisioning view 
Plot every SKU on a stockout-versus-overstock grid. The four quadrants map directly to actions, so 
the ops team can triage hundreds of SKUs at a glance instead of reading a table row by row. 
 
Figure 6.  Decisioning view — each SKU placed on a stockout vs overstock grid, sized by revenue at stake.

09   Deliverable Specifications 
 
Each deliverable has acceptance criteria — the client’s definition of done. Treat them as your 
checklist; mentors grade against them. 
D1   Data pipeline 
Acceptance criteria 
1. Ingests all four extracts and produces one analysis-ready dataset. 
2. Cleaning steps (missing values, duplicates, type fixes) are coded, not manual. 
3. Re-runs end-to-end from raw files with a single command. 
4. Key cleaning decisions are documented with their rationale. 
 
D2   Data-quality & EDA insight memo 
Acceptance criteria 
1. Reports data-quality issues found and how they were handled. 
2. Shows demand patterns: seasonality, trend, top movers, and dead stock. 
3. States at least three business-relevant insights in plain language. 
4. Charts are labelled and readable by a non-technical reader. 
 
D3   Demand forecast model 
Acceptance criteria 
1. Produces a weekly SKU-level forecast over the defined horizon. 
2. Includes a seasonal-naive baseline for comparison. 
3. Backtested with rolling-origin CV; reports WAPE vs the baseline. 
4. No data leakage — future information never enters a feature. 
 
D4   Risk scoring 
Acceptance criteria 
1. Scores stockout and overstock risk for every SKU over the horizon. 
2. Attaches a recommended action and the rupee value at stake. 
3. Logic is transparent and explainable, not a black box. 
4. Output reconciles with the decisioning grid in Section 08. 
 
D5   Planning dashboard 
Acceptance criteria 
1. Filterable by category and SKU; shows forecast vs actual and risk flags. 
2. Surfaces a prioritised reorder / markdown list. 
3. Loads on seeded data and is usable by a non-technical person. 
4. Has clear empty and loading states.

Risk Mitigation 
Sparse history for new SKUs Fall back to category-level patterns; flag low-confidence

Promotions distort demand Include promo features; report forecasts with and without promo

• Document every assumption and cleaning decision — the client must be able to audit the work. 
17   Originality & Professional Conduct 
 
• You may use AI assistants, libraries, and documentation — that is normal practice. You must 
understand and be able to explain every result you present. 
• You may reference tutorials, but the analysis, modelling choices, and risk logic must be your 
own work on this dataset. 
• Never fabricate results or hide a poor backtest. Reporting that the baseline won is honest and

19   Appendix B — Metric Definitions 
 
Metric Definition & why it is used 
WAPE 
Weighted Absolute Percentage Error — total absolute error divided by total actual 
demand. Primary accuracy metric; robust to low-volume SKUs where MAPE 
explodes. 
MAPE Mean Absolute Percentage Error — reported as a secondary, intuitive figure, but 
unreliable when demand is near zero. 
Bias Mean forecast error (signed) — checks whether the model systematically over- or 
under-forecasts.

21   Appendix D — Glossary 
 
Term Meaning 
SKU Stock-Keeping Unit — a single sellable product variant. 
Stockout Running out of stock so demand cannot be met — lost sales. 
Overstock Holding far more stock than will sell — locked capital and markdown risk. 
Lead time Time between ordering stock and it arriving and being sellable. 
Reorder point Stock level at which a replenishment order should be placed. 
Backtest Testing a forecast on history it did not train on, to estimate real accuracy. 
Baseline A simple reference forecast a real model must beat to be worth using. 
Working capital Cash tied up in operations — including stock sitting in the warehouse.
